Long-Term Care Planning Basics

Most long-term care isn't covered by Medicare. Here's what typically is, what isn't, and when to start planning.

Long-term care refers to the ongoing help some people need with everyday activities — bathing, dressing, meal preparation, or supervision due to memory conditions — whether at home, in an assisted living community, or in a nursing facility. It's one of the most misunderstood pieces of retirement planning, largely because of a common assumption that Medicare will cover it. In most cases, it doesn't.

What Medicare does and doesn't cover

Medicare covers short-term skilled nursing or rehabilitation care following a qualifying hospital stay, but it is not designed to pay for the kind of ongoing custodial care (help with daily activities) that most long-term care involves. This is one of the biggest gaps in retirement planning, since extended care needs can last months or years.

Why the cost matters

Extended care — whether in-home care, assisted living, or a nursing facility — can be one of the largest unplanned expenses in retirement. Because these costs can accumulate quickly, many people find that without a plan in place, long-term care needs can significantly draw down savings that were intended for other retirement goals.

Options worth understanding

Traditional long-term care insurance is purchased specifically to cover extended care costs, typically at lower premiums when purchased earlier and in good health. Hybrid life insurance/long-term care policies combine a death benefit with the ability to access funds for long-term care if needed, which appeals to people who want to avoid a "use it or lose it" feeling. There are also state partnership programs and other structures worth understanding depending on where you live.

When to start planning

Long-term care coverage is generally easier to qualify for — and less expensive — earlier in life, while you're in good health. Many people begin exploring options in their 50s or early 60s, well before a need arises, rather than waiting until a health event makes planning more difficult or costly.
