Life Insurance in Retirement — Do You Still Need It?

Your need for life insurance often changes in retirement. Here's how to think through what makes sense now.

Many people first buy life insurance to replace income during their working years — to protect a spouse or children if something happened unexpectedly. By the time retirement arrives, that original reason for coverage may no longer apply, which leads many retirees to wonder whether they still need a policy at all. For some, the answer is no. For others, life insurance takes on a new and different purpose.

Reasons life insurance can still matter

Even after your working years, life insurance can serve several purposes. It can provide for a surviving spouse who depends on your pension or Social Security income, since those benefits are often reduced or lost entirely when one spouse passes away. It can also cover final expenses — funeral costs, medical bills, or outstanding debts — so those costs don't fall on your family during an already difficult time. For some retirees, life insurance is also part of a legacy or estate plan, providing a tax-free benefit to children, grandchildren, or a charitable cause.

When it may make sense to reduce or drop coverage

If the original reason you bought a policy no longer applies — for example, your children are financially independent and your mortgage is paid off — you may be carrying more coverage (and paying more in premium) than you actually need. This is worth reviewing periodically rather than assuming your original policy still fits your current situation.

Term vs. permanent coverage in retirement

If you have a term policy that's nearing the end of its term, it's worth evaluating whether to let it expire, convert it, or replace it with a smaller permanent policy sized to your current needs (such as final expenses). Permanent, or whole life, policies build cash value over time and never expire, which some retirees value for legacy planning even if the original income-replacement need has passed.

How to think it through

The right answer depends on who depends on your income, what debts or expenses would fall on your family, and what legacy goals matter to you. Reviewing this as part of your broader retirement plan — rather than in isolation — usually leads to a clearer answer.
